Introduction: From Scarcity to Cognitive Abundance (5 Minutes)
For nearly two decades, I’ve served in executive leadership within higher education in the United States, working with both large university systems and historic institutions. Along the way, my work has centered on how we learn, how we teach, and how we prepare people for a rapidly changing future. More recently, I had the opportunity to help launch the first fully AI-based university campus in the U.S., an experience that reshaped how I understand the partnership between human thinking and artificial intelligence.

I began my journey in Romania, earning a degree in computer science from Lucian Blaga University in Sibiu. Today, I serve as President of Life College International here in Manila, where we are developing new AI-driven approaches to learning. My doctoral work focused on AI in education, leading to the development of a framework called Neogogy, designed for a world where AI is not just a tool, but a partner in thinking.
I share this simply to say I’ve had the privilege of seeing both the opportunities and the challenges this moment presents. We are moving from an age of information scarcity to one of cognitive abundance. Unlike previous innovations, AI does not just give us access to knowledge, it reshapes how thinking itself happens. And for those of us called to lead, teach, and disciple, this raises an urgent question. How do we faithfully form people in a world where intelligence itself is being transformed?

I have visited Mastercard's headquarters in Singapore and stood inside their Innovation Lab, looking at data projecting a $1.8 trillion AI-driven market by 2030, where 20 percent of all customer interactions will be managed by autonomous AI agents. They said our wives will be walking in the store and pay with facial recognition… I said I support burkas and face covering at that point. 

We are transitioning from an era of information scarcity to one of cognitive abundance. The printing press, digital libraries, and online education addressed issues of access; they made existing knowledge cheaper and easier to distribute. Artificial intelligence is entirely different. It automates the core processes of thinking, reasoning, synthesis, and writing. This is a difference not of extent, but of kind. And as pastors, leaders, and educators, if we fail to rethink our approach to pedagogy, discipleship, and human formation in the age of artificial intelligence, we fail to speak in the new “marketplace” of humanity. 

"The heart of the discerning acquires knowledge, for the ears of the wise seek it out." (Proverbs 18:15, NLT)
Part I: The Genesis and Reality of Artificial Intelligence (10 Minutes)
Before we can understand the theological and pastoral implications of artificial intelligence, we must define what it actually is and how we arrived at this precise moment in history. The conversation around AI is often clouded by science fiction and hyperbole, but the reality is both more grounded and, in many ways, more profound.
At its core, artificial intelligence is the simulation of human intelligence processes by computer systems. It is the ability of a machine to perform tasks that typically require human cognition, such as learning, reasoning, problem-solving, perception, and language understanding. However, it is crucial to understand that not all AI is created equal. We generally categorize AI into three distinct levels of capability.

The first is Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI), often referred to as "Weak AI." This is the AI we interact with every single day. It is designed to perform a single task or a narrow set of tasks exceptionally well. When your email filters out spam, when Netflix recommends a movie, or when you ask Siri about the weather, you are interacting with Narrow AI. It operates under a limited, pre-defined set of constraints.

The second level is Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), or "Strong AI." This represents a machine with the ability to understand, learn, and apply intelligence across a broad range of tasks, at a level equal to a human being. We have not yet achieved AGI, but it is the stated goal of major research laboratories like OpenAI and DeepMind. AGI would possess cognitive flexibility, able to transfer learning from one domain to an entirely unrelated one.

The final theoretical level is Artificial Superintelligence (ASI). This describes a scenario where machine intellect surpasses human intelligence across all fields, including scientific creativity, general wisdom, and social skills. This is the realm that prompts the most significant ethical and existential questions.

How did we get here? The journey of AI is not a recent phenomenon; it is the culmination of decades of mathematical and computational pursuit. 

The conceptual foundation was laid in 1950 when British mathematician Alan Turing published his seminal paper, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," introducing the "Imitation Game," now known as the Turing Test, to determine if a machine could exhibit intelligent behavior indistinguishable from a human. 
The term "Artificial Intelligence" itself was coined shortly after, in 1956, by computer scientist John McCarthy at the historic Dartmouth Conference.
For decades, AI progressed through cycles of high optimism followed by periods of reduced funding and interest, known as "AI winters." However, key milestones continually pushed the boundaries. In 1997, IBM's Deep Blue defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov, proving that machines could master highly complex, rule-based systems. In 2016, Google's AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol, a world champion in the ancient game of Go, a feat previously thought to be decades away because Go requires intuition and pattern recognition, not just brute-force calculation.

The true inflection point, the moment that birthed the current era of generative AI, occurred in 2017 with the introduction of the "Transformer" architecture by Google researchers. 

This breakthrough fundamentally changed how machines process human language. Instead of reading words sequentially, transformers analyze entire sentences at once, understanding the context and relationships between words with unprecedented accuracy. This architecture is the "T" in ChatGPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer).

When OpenAI released ChatGPT in November 2022, it was not just a technological milestone; it was a cultural earthquake. It reached 100 million active users in just two months, making it the fastest-growing consumer application in history. We moved from an era where computers calculated numbers to an era where computers generate language, write code, compose poetry, and synthesize complex ideas. We have built machines that, while devoid of consciousness, are highly proficient at mimicking human cognition. This reality forces us to ask a fundamental question: if a machine can perform the cognitive tasks we once believed were uniquely human, what does it actually mean to be human?

"What are mere mortals that you should think about them, human beings that you should care for them? Yet you made them only a little lower than God and crowned them with glory and honor." (Psalm 8:4-5, NLT)
Part II: Technology, Faith, and the Imago Dei (25 Minutes)
As we navigate this landscape of cognitive abundance, we must anchor ourselves in a robust theology of technology. The intersection of faith and artificial intelligence is not merely an ethical debate about data privacy or job displacement; it is a profoundly theological conversation about human identity, the Imago Dei, and our relationship with the Creator.
The Nature of Technology: Amplifier and Response
To understand AI, we must first understand technology itself. One of the most dangerous things we can believe in this world is that technology is neutral. As theologian John Dyer notes, "Technology is the means by which we transform the world as it is into the world that we desire. What we often fail to notice is that it is not only the world that gets transformed by technology. We, too, are transformed."
Christian thinker James Spencer provides a vital framework for this in his extensive writings. He argues that technology is fundamentally an amplifier of human capacity. "Because technology amplifies human capacities," Spencer writes, "the relatively unchecked power of the digital world will allow us to produce good and evil at scale." Technology does not create new human problems; it amplifies old ones. Because we possess the capacity for both truth and falsehood, beauty and ugliness, we cannot expect technology to solve what is fundamentally a spiritual condition.
If we look back to Genesis, we see that human capacity was maximized when it was exercised in dependence on, and under the authority of, God. Before the Fall, Adam and Eve lived in an ordered reality. But after the Fall, human capacity was diminished. Spencer points out that the human couple's sudden awareness of their nakedness was "not an initial discovery but the realization that they are now vulnerable because they are naked." In response to this vulnerability, they sewed fig leaves together. This was the first technology, a provisional human response to the brokenness and vulnerability of a post-Fall world.
"Then the eyes of both of them were opened, and they realized they were naked; so they sewed fig leaves together and made coverings for themselves." (Genesis 3:7, NLT)

Therefore, technology is intended to help us deal with life in a fallen world. In many cases, we need it to survive. Tony Reinke reminds us that "God continues to use human technologies both to judge and to bless humanity. Babel and Golgotha force us to see the complexity of God's sovereign relationship to our tools." Yet, this provisional tool carries a profound temptation. As Spencer warns, "Technology... can drive us toward self-sufficiency when we need to realize that being dependent on and loyal to God is what makes the world work." It tempts us to set aside faithfulness for efficiency, trusting in our own amplified capacity and leaving God behind.
"Trust in the Lord with all your heart; do not depend on your own understanding. Seek his will in all you do, and he will show you which path to take." (Proverbs 3:5-6, NLT)
The Imago Dei in a Digital Age
This brings us to the core question of human identity. When machines can write sermons, generate art, and pass the bar exam, what makes us uniquely human? Jason Thacker addresses this directly: "According to the Biblical Scriptures, humanity is distinct from the rest of creation because we were created in the likeness of God. The basis of the uniqueness and dignity of humans isn't based on what we do, how we think, or any kind of intrinsic part of ourselves... we were created in God's likeness to represent him."
Being made in the image of God is not about possessing a specific cognitive skill set that AI might one day replicate. It is about a relational vocation. Spencer emphasizes that "what it means to be human is not dependent on technology and technology has the potential to diminish our humanity." Bearing God's image requires our unqualified allegiance to the Lord. It is a calling to exercise dominion, not as a license for self-assertion, but as a vocation given within the context of God's blessing and under His authority.
"So God created human beings in his own image. In the image of God he created them; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27, NLT)
Oxford mathematician and Christian apologist John Lennox captures the stark contrast between the secular tech narrative and the Christian gospel: "Man thinks he can become God. But infinitely greater than that is the fact that God thought of becoming human." The transhumanist desire to merge with machines or achieve immortality through AI is a modern Tower of Babel. As Spencer notes regarding Babel, God's concern was not merely their pride, but their capacity exercised apart from Him: "Apart from God's constraints, humans will always produce a skewed vision of God because experiencing God requires obedience."
"Look, the people are united, and they all speak the same language. After this, nothing they set out to do will be impossible for them!" (Genesis 11:6, NLT)
The Illusion of Disembodied Connection
One of the most subtle and pervasive dangers of the digital age is how it redefines love and presence. AI and digital platforms promise connection, but it is often a disembodied, low-effort simulation. Cultural critic Andy Crouch observes that "technology does not exist primarily, and never existed primarily, to serve us or support 'ordinary embodied human existence.'" Instead, it often displaces genuine human connection.
Spencer illustrates this by reimagining the Parable of the Good Samaritan for the digital age. "Being a neighbor requires effort, contact, a closing of the distance between us and others. It requires us to be present with others," he writes. However, "Digital culture often treats symbolic gestures as the moral equivalent of costly action. While digital tools can amplify acts of mercy, they can also encourage a low-cost, at-a-distance sort of care that frees us from the inconveniences often associated with bearing one another's burdens."
We see this in our increasing reliance on AI for communication. When we use AI to draft emails to our congregants, write our apologies, or mediate our conflicts, we are outsourcing our compassion. Theologian Noreen Herzfeld warns that her overarching concern is not that AI will become malevolent, "but that we might forget what makes us human, namely, our capacity to love, to suffer, and to engage in genuine mutual relationship." True relationship requires embodiment, reciprocal communication, mutual assistance, and emotional intentionality. AI can simulate empathy, but it cannot suffer with you. It can generate comforting words, but it cannot bear your burdens.
"Dear children, let's not merely say that we love each other; let us show the truth by our actions." (1 John 3:18, NLT)
As Jay Kim writes in Analog Christian, "Pop-culture love is a chasing after bubbles. Love as vocation is gardening. And it is love as vocation, getting our hands in the dirt, patiently and methodically going about the work of receiving and giving love... that eventually and inevitably delivers us from our self-centric despair."
Cultivating a Theological Disposition
How, then, do we live faithfully in this environment? We must recognize that the digital world is constantly discipling us. It presents what Spencer calls a "plausible digital narrative" that replaces reality itself. "A map was becoming the terrain," he observes. The digital world rewards exaggerated responses, emotive content, and speed over accuracy. It pushes us toward what Jean Baudrillard called "hyperreality," representations of reality that are entirely detached from God.
"Don't copy the behavior and customs of this world, but let God transform you into a new person by changing the way you think. Then you will learn to know God's will for you, which is good and pleasing and perfect." (Romans 12:2, NLT)

If we consume this content uncritically, we run the risk of "learning to embody a pseudo-reality that calls us to reorder our loves, reorient our attention, and respond to the call of the world rather than the voice of God." To counter this, Spencer argues that Christians must cultivate a "theological disposition." This is an internalized sense of how the world works that is fundamentally rooted in God's preeminence.
This disposition requires us to reorder our loves, nesting all our affections within our supreme love for God. It requires us to reorient our attention, recognizing that where we direct our focus is a theological act. And it requires us to develop a "Christian habitus," a durable pattern of responding to God as the primary actor in every situation, rather than merely reacting to the outrage of the day.

To aid in this discernment, Spencer offers a framework called the "Quad," adapted from cognitive psychology, which involves four ways of knowing:

1.Propositional: Knowing that something is true.
2.Procedural: Knowing how to do something.
3.Perspectival: Knowing from a specific point of view.
4.Participatory: Knowing by being, conforming to reality's claim on us.
"The Quad is not simply a method for critical reflection," Spencer notes. "It is a reflective discipleship practice." It slows down our thinking in a digital world that demands instant reactions, allowing us to engage information with a calm, thoughtful, and theological posture.
Affirming the True, the Good, and the Beautiful
Finally, our theological engagement with technology must not be purely defensive. We are called to recognize and affirm Truth (Veritas), Goodness (Bonum), and Beauty (Pulchrum) wherever they are found, because all truth is God's truth.
The modern world often tries to co-opt these transcendentals. "Truth is not seen as having a claim on humanity," Spencer writes, "but as something to be managed, curated, and used to achieve a desired end." The world recognizes fragments of truth, goodness, and beauty, but fails to acknowledge the Triune God as their source.
"And you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free." (John 8:32, NLT)

As Christians, we do not determine what is true, good, or beautiful; we recognize them in the fullest sense. We must adopt a posture of "disciplined openness," expecting to encounter traces of God's goodness even in unexpected places, perhaps even in the marvels of artificial intelligence. Yet, we must always point beyond the fragment to the Source. "Critique is necessary, but critique alone is insufficient," Spencer concludes. "Christians are called not only to expose falsehood and distortion, but to testify to God's ongoing presence and activity in the world."
Part III: The Future of Work and Education (10 Minutes)
As we establish our theological grounding, we must look squarely at the practical realities reshaping our world. The urgency of this transformation is driven by massive shifts in the external labor market. We are no longer just dealing with chatbots that can write essays; we are entering the era of "Agentic AI." These are intelligent systems that operate independently to perform complex, multi-step tasks across different fields without human prompting.
Recall my visit to the Mastercard Innovation Lab in Singapore. The data is staggering. By 2030, the AI-driven customer experience market is projected to reach $1.8 trillion. But the most disruptive revelation was the shift toward "Self-Driving Commerce." By 2030, 20 percent of customer interactions are expected to be managed entirely by autonomous AI agents. We are looking at AI negotiating business-to-business contracts, managing global supply chains, and executing predictive fraud detection.

This fundamentally changes the landscape for our congregants and our students. Graduates will no longer just compete against other humans for jobs; they will collaborate with and compete against autonomous AI agents. An education system, or a discipleship model, that only verifies time spent or facts memorized will leave people dangerously unprepared for an economy where machines can perform routine cognitive tasks instantly and more efficiently than humans.
"The wise are mightier than the strong, and those with knowledge grow stronger and stronger." (Proverbs 24:5, NLT)

In higher education, the traditional model is broken. The university lecture was originally an ingenious economic fix for the scarcity of handwritten books in the Middle Ages. It was a technology for spreading scarce knowledge. Today, we have moved from information scarcity to cognitive abundance. AI automates the core processes of thinking, reasoning, synthesis, and writing that have been central to academic learning for centuries.

When a student can produce a complex research paper with a simple prompt, and a professor uses a similar AI tool to grade that paper, we enter a hollowed-out environment where students pretend to learn and faculty pretend to teach. The proxy for learning has failed. As I wrote in my recent paper, "The most deadly sign of institutional failure is not enrollment decline or financial stress. It is the failure to rethink pedagogy in the age of artificial intelligence."

This is why I developed the Neogogy framework. It is a pedagogical model that treats AI not as a threat to be banned, but as a "partner in cognition." If AI can generate standard answers instantly, the focus of human education must shift toward uniquely human skills. We must transition our schools and our churches from being "institutions of transmission" to "ecosystems of cognition."
Neogogy is built on seven core principles, but at its heart, it is about cultivating individuals capable of independent thought, moral reasoning, intellectual courage, and the kind of creative synthesis that only comes from genuine human experience. 
In a world flooded with digital intelligence and algorithmic efficiency, the most valuable experiences will be those that are most human at their core. We must prepare true learners for a world with agentic AI, equipping them to adapt quickly while remaining deeply anchored in their humanity.
"Intelligent people are always ready to learn. Their ears are open for knowledge." (Proverbs 18:15, NLT)


Part IV: The Role of the Pastor in the Age of AI (5 Minutes)
What does all of this mean for us as pastors? How do we lead our congregations through this unprecedented cognitive and technological shift?

The temptation is to either embrace AI uncritically as a church growth hack or to reject it entirely out of fear. Both are failures of pastoral leadership. Pastor and missiologist Todd Korpi aptly notes, "AI deals in knowledge, humans deal in wisdom. Speed is the enemy of wisdom, discernment, and, often, human flourishing." Our role is not to out-compute AI, but to out-human it. Our calling is to cultivate wisdom in an age drowning in information.

We live in a high-choice media environment that fragments Christian thinking. James Spencer warns that this environment leads to "compartmentalization" (relegating faith to the private sphere) and "syncretism" (diluting faith to serve worldly aims). The result is a dis-integrated church. Spencer argues powerfully that "Discipleship is crucial to such resistance because it is the church's coordinating mechanism."

As pastors, our primary response to the age of AI is not technological; it is theological. It is robust, embodied discipleship. Here are four profound, challenging takeaways for positioning our pastoral leadership in the age of AI:
"Be shepherds of God's flock that is under your care, watching over them, not because you must, but because you are willing, as God wants you to be." (1 Peter 5:2, NLT)

1.We Must Become Architects of Embodied Community. AI can simulate empathy, but it cannot offer physical presence. It cannot lay hands on the sick, weep with those who weep, or share the Eucharist. We must intentionally design our churches to be "Communities of Recognition" (as Andy Crouch calls them), households where people are known, needed, and loved in the flesh. Let us resist the urge to outsource pastoral care to digital efficiencies. Let us lead our congregations into the beautiful, messy inconvenience of physical proximity.
"For where two or three gather together as my followers, I am there among them." (Matthew 18:20, NLT)

2. We Must Shift from Information Delivery to Wisdom Formation. If our preaching is merely the transmission of biblical facts, an AI can do it better and faster. But an AI cannot preach from a place of brokenness, repentance, and the ongoing experience of the Holy Spirit. Our sermons must move beyond information transfer to the formation of a "theological disposition." We need to teach our people how to think theologically, not just what to think, utilizing frameworks like the Quad to slow down their thinking and foster deep discernment.
"If you need wisdom, ask our generous God, and he will give it to you. He will not rebuke you for asking." (James 1:5, NLT)

3.We Must Disciple the Imagination, Not Just the Intellect. The digital world is constantly telling compelling stories that distance us from God. We must offer a more compelling narrative. We need to disciple our people to recognize Truth, Goodness, and Beauty in the world, while always pointing beyond the fragments to the Triune God. Let us teach them that technology is a provisional tool for a broken world, not a savior that will usher in a utopia.
"Fix your thoughts on what is true, and honorable, and right, and pure, and lovely, and admirable. Think about things that are excellent and worthy of praise." (Philippians 4:8, NLT)

4. We Must Model 'Disciplined Openness' and Restraint. In a digital culture that rewards instant, outraged reactions, the most radical pastoral witness is calm, thoughtful, theological restraint. Let us demonstrate what it looks like to live under the authority of Christ by speaking boldly but carefully, responding to God rather than reacting to the news cycle, and focusing on glorifying God rather than attempting to control the world.
"Understand this, my dear brothers and sisters: You must all be quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to get angry." (James 1:19, NLT)
Conclusion
We are not the first generation of Christians to face massive technological upheaval. From the printing press to the industrial revolution, the Church has navigated the shifting tectonic plates of human innovation. But this moment is unique because it touches the very core of cognition and identity.
John Dyer reminds us that "God is more interested in our theology of worship than in our technology of worship." As we return to our congregations, let us not fear the rise of intelligent machines. Instead, let us guard against the possibility that the people of God might buy the lie that we are nothing more than machines ourselves.

Let us lead with courage. Let us cultivate deep, embodied discipleship. Let us partner with technology where it aids human flourishing, but fiercely protect the sacred, relational vocation of bearing the Imago Dei.
"For I know the plans I have for you," says the Lord. "They are plans for good and not for disaster, to give you a future and a hope." (Jeremiah 29:11, NLT)
Thank you.
