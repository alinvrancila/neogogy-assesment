Uncoordinated: The Need for Discipled Content Creation and Consumption

Introduction
We live in a world that has become increasingly proficient at fragmenting our thinking so that it becomes more challenging to develop a cohesive sense of what it means to be Christian and what it means to be Christian together. To be Christian together is not merely to share a confession, but to share an understanding of how we are to inhabit the world in ways shaped by that confession. When that shared understanding—what I will call coordination—breaks down, Christians may still agree on doctrine while disagreeing about how a given doctrine should be understood and worked out in practice. 
Fragmentation opposes shared understanding by creating the illusion that God’s word is irrelevant—or at least nonauthoritative—to particular areas of life. In doing so, it allows Christians to affirm God’s authority in principle while functionally setting it aside in practice. The result is not disbelief, but dis-integration. It produces a fractured way of thinking about and inhabiting the world, undermining the church’s ability to live and witness together. 
Educational, political, economic, and religious systems—even Christian ones—have always extended their capacity through technology. The emergence of a “high choice media environment” has created intensified the competition for our attention. As Christians, we have always had choices about what to consume. We are not simply subject to our environment, but we are influenced by it as shared narratives frame what appears plausible within a given cultural context. Our high-choice media environment is the logical outworking of what we already believe about ourselves and the world, rather than a neutral development imposed upon us. We generally assume this environment’s upsides outweigh its downsides, even as we acknowledge the challenges it poses. In the United States, the high-choice media environment is grounded in a shared narrative of “expressive individualism,” in which “each person has a unique core of feeling and intuition that should unfold or be expressed if individuality is to be realized.”

Because it predates digital technology by centuries, our high-choice media environment emerges from a deeper narrative that elevates individual uniqueness while marginalizing traditional sources of identity, such as religion. This way of making sense of the world is widely shared yet largely inconspicuous. It is ever-present but relatively unnoticed apart from its effects. Our shared narrative, however subtle, reveals itself in the way we speak, construe the events of the world, and decide what is relevant. As we are shaped into women and men who commit ourselves to the ways of the world, we become disciples of a way other than Jesus (Jn 14:6; cf. Acts 9:2; Rom 12:2). 

It is not so much that we abandon our faith in Christ as that we compartmentalize it, as if other areas of life lay outside of God’s authority, and merge it with incompatible views, as if our theological convictions serve only to legitimate our own ideas rather than determine them (i.e., syncretism). We are tempted to fit our faith within some other shared narrative, and in doing so our faith fragments. In doing so, Christ’s body becomes uncoordinated, or, more precisely, coordinated around a shared narrative that denies and distorts the Triune God. Our high-choice media environment reinforces this fragmentation not as its root cause, but by exerting a significant formative influence that amplifies and normalizes these patterns of compartmentalization and syncretism.

Compartmentalism and Syncretism
Compartmentalization and syncretism may be understood as mechanisms of fragmentation that disciple us in the ways of authorities claiming to be more complete than they actually are. Compartmentalization relegates our faith to the private sphere while syncretism dilutes faith by making it serve other aims rather than govern them. These interconnected mechanisms of fragmentation distort God’s authority by immersing the church’s theological convictions within a symbolic world governed by overreaching authorities that do not acknowledge the limits set by the Triune God.

Because nothing falls outside God’s authority, compartmentalization necessarily involves syncretism and vice versa. Scripture refuses to recognize any sphere of life as autonomous from God and, as such, insists that no sphere is theologically neutral. We are not tempted to deny God outright, but to restrict him. Like Israel, we are tempted to recognize God in worship while ignoring him in our everyday lives (Isa 1:11-17; 58:3-7; Amos 5:21-23; Zech 7:5-6). 

Compartmentalization is no minor theological misstep. By creating spaces where we ignore God’s authority, we offer a false witness and fail to love God with all we are and have. When faith is limited to the private sphere, we narrow the scope of God’s authority. 

Though our preferences cannot truly limit God, in practice we attempt to restrict him, even if we rarely abandon him altogether. We seek to fuse our faith with other religious, cultural, or philosophical commitments, allowing those rival sources of meaning to govern the areas of life from which God has been implicitly excluded. Compartmentalization creates the space in which syncretism flourishes, and syncretism reinforces the divisions that make compartmentalization seem plausible, necessary, and, at times, good. 

To illustrate the dynamics of compartmentalization and syncretism, consider sociologist Robert Bellah’s description of American civil religion (ACR):

“The American civil religion was never anticlerical or militantly secular. On the contrary, it borrowed selectively from the religious tradition in such a way that the average American saw no conflict between the two. In this way, the civil religion was able to build up without any bitter struggle with the church powerful symbols of national solidarity and to mobilize deep levels of personal motivation for the attainment of national goals.” 

Bellah assumes compartmentalization by suggesting that ACR was not opposed to other religions, which were free to exist, worship, and retain distinctive beliefs. Those involved in developing and sustaining ACR did not wish to eliminate Christianity because it was going to incorporate it. By borrowing from other religions, ACR appeared as a friend, even as it emptied Christian language of its theological content and reoriented it toward national ends. Continuing to allow the separate sphere (compartmentalization) while cultivating an amenable relationship with it (syncretism) served the development of ACR by allowing Christianity to remain distinct and separate while, at least at times, functionally and unwittingly subordinate.

The theological significance of ACR involves its borrowing of Christian language and its claim on Christians more generally. When Christian symbols, emptied of their theological content, are redirected toward national ends, God is no longer recognized as authoritative. The state’s distorted rendering of God becomes the state’s tool. ACR denies the Triune God and creates a domesticated god dressed in American garb. Civil religion provides a clear example of how compartmentalization and syncretism can coexist. Christianity and its worship are allowed to occupy a specific realm (compartmentalization) while Christian language and symbols are incorporated into the worship of the state (syncretism). 

Within a high-choice media environment that reinforces and encourages fragmentation, compartmentalization and syncretism are not accidental failures of the system but essential features of its operation. Faith is not absent, but dispersed across incompatible narratives. While such narratives assert their coherence and ability to make sense of the world, they deny the Triune God’s authority.
Resisting this pattern is therefore part of what it means to be the church. Discipleship is crucial to such resistance because it is the church’s coordinating mechanism. It cultivates our shared sense of the way God’s creation works and how we are to live in the presence of God. Without such coordination, the church’s witness will be confused and confusing, not because Christ is unclear, but because his people are uncoordinated. They stray into a world of choices, embrace beliefs and practices incommensurate with Christian faith, and begin to speak the language of the world—albeit with a Christian accent. Christians must do more than occasionally object to the world’s ways; we must coordinate together to form an alternative pattern that reflects the self-giving life of our crucified and risen Savior.

Coordination, Common Knowledge, and Discipleship
Coordination depends on common knowledge. By common knowledge, I do not simply mean information held in common, but shared understanding—knowing what others know, how they understand it, and how they are likely to act on it. To coordinate with others, we need to know what others know and that others know, as well as participating in a common “symbolic system” that allows us to know how others understand what is known. 

As such, common knowledge cannot be reduced to doctrinal affirmation. Doctrinal commitments are necessary but insufficient when divorced from a shared symbolic system, because that symbolic system is what assigns meaning to the words we use and the knowledge we possess. What we know is similar to the words we speak because, as Niklas Forsberg suggests, words can be “changed but not necessarily exchanged,” since identical terms may carry different concepts. For instance, two Christians may affirm that “Jesus is Lord” while operating with radically different understandings of what lordship entails. For one, it may primarily name a private spiritual allegiance; for another, it may order political judgment, economic practice, and moral responsibility. The confession is shared, but the symbolic world that gives it meaning is not. Without this shared understanding, coordination becomes difficult, if not impossible.

Discipleship serves as the church’s coordinating mechanism, in part, because it immerses us within a symbolic system—which, for the church, is necessarily theological—through which authority, meaning, and action are ordered under Christ. Though we often think about what we know (e.g., doctrine, biblical verses, etc.), how we understand what we know is equally crucial because coordination can’t happen without it. If we adopt a strictly procedural or instrumental logic, we can’t coordinate as God’s people. We must instead embrace a Theo-logic through which we transcend “the limits of life on earth,” set aside strictly “this-worldly criteria,” and learn to “accept all things with the mind as related, directly or indirectly, to man’s eternal destiny as the redeemed and chosen child of God.”

Compartmentalization and syncretism undermine the church’s coordination by decoupling shared knowledge from shared understanding and severing both from shared practices. This too often leaves Christians with the façade of a shared theological world. That façade is incapable of coordinating God’s people for faithful witness. Coordination becomes possible when God’s people hold similar beliefs, know that others within the community of faith also hold them, understand those beliefs in sufficiently similar ways, and reasonably expect others to act in line with that understanding. 

A high-choice media environment allows us to reinforce divergent ways of understanding the world, because it enables us to consume or create content that undermines Christian coordination. Not all Christian content stifles coordination, but certain forms of content play directly into the dynamics of compartmentalization, syncretism, and fragmentation rather than encouraging unity. Content that undermines coordination tends to share a common feature: it uses God’s word to justify the pursuit of aims other than the glorification of God. Three types of content are worth highlighting here: (1) activistic, (2) ambulance-chasing, and (3) institutionalized content. These content forms differ not merely in tone, but in their governing logic: activism is ends-driven, ambulance-chasing is attention-driven, and institutionalized content is survival-driven. Each of these forms trains Christians to interpret faithfulness through a logic other than obedience to Christ.

First, activistic content calls Christians to commit to some cause. It seeks to mobilize God’s people to effect some change in the world. The concern becomes less about being formed in Christ than about accomplishing a particular aim via the world’s means. That aim is often framed not merely as permissible, but as essential to faithful Christian practice.

For instance, while attending an event addressing Christians and politics, a number of the speakers espoused Christian nationalism and encouraged Christians to vote (or, though it wasn’t explicit, to vote Republican). At one point, Ralph Reed, former head of the Christian Coalition, noted the unprecedented ability “to identify and turn out the largest number of Christian, Bible believing, born again Christian voters literally in the history of our country.” He went on to suggest that not using the available “technological innovations” to mobilize these believers “is spiritual as well as civic malpractice” because we would be neglecting an opportunity for “building…God’s kingdom here on earth.” Christians should care about the world, but they cannot redefine faithfulness in terms of effectiveness rather than patient and obedient endurance.

While Reed’s comments might seem innocent enough, he elevates the importance of Christian political engagement, making it essential to Christian practice. “Spiritual and civic malpractice” assumes Christian voting is a moral, if not theological, imperative. Voting is certainly a right Christians may or may not choose to exercise, but it is not intrinsic to Christian practice. In doing so, activistic content trains Christians to recognize one another primarily as resources for achieving an end rather than as fellow disciples being formed under Christ’s authority.

Reed’s symbolic world becomes more apparent when he ties the mobilization of Christian voters to “building…God’s kingdom here on earth.” Here, we begin to see the fragmentation of shared understanding. Reed understands political action as a means of advancing God’s agenda in the here and now. However, in biblical and theological perspective, it isn’t at all clear that Christian political participation is capable of “building…God’s kingdom here on earth.” Instead, Christian political engagement may pursue good outcomes through mechanisms that diminish God’s glory. 
The New Testament does not present the kingdom of God in terms of near-term political action or expanded influence. Jesus announces a kingdom that arrives through faithful suffering, witness, and resurrection, not through political leverage (Mk 8:34-35; Jn 18:36). God’s kingdom has public implications, but it is not advanced by the mechanisms that govern earthly power because it does not struggle against “flesh and blood, but against the rulers, against the authorities, against the cosmic powers over this present darkness, against the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly places” (Eph 6:12). 

Were political effectiveness to become a measure of the church’s success, the church would abandon the logic of the cross and give up the battle that calls it to “take up the whole armor of God” (6:13). The result is misplaced zeal fueled by a reordered imagination in which political influence displaces obedience as the primary mark of Christian fidelity. As I suggest in Serpents and Doves, “If we have become too enamored with our ability to ‘control’ the course of our nation through political means, we run the risk of misrepresenting God to the world and, at the same time, losing our Theo-political advantage.” 

Activistic content is not limited to politics. It can also encourage us to “take up” technological, educational, financial, or social armor in order to build “God’s kingdom here on earth.” Such content fragments the church not because it exposes sin, but because it trains Christians to relate to the body of Christ primarily as a means of mobilization rather than as accountable members of Christ’s body.

Second, ambulance-chasing content centers on scandals, emergencies, and offenses—whether real or imagined—and treats them as essential to sustaining attention and influence. While it is possible for this sort of content to be contained within a single article, video, or podcast, the challenge lies with “ambulance chasers” themselves and the audiences who support them. Ambulance chasers often legitimate their efforts with appeals to accountability and justice, leading those who consume their content to believe this sort of content to be a viable, if not essential, mechanism for building up the body of Christ. 

This sort of content is particularly dangerous because verifiable scandals within the Christian community give ambulance chasers an air of credibility, allowing them to publish stories that are at best speculative and at worst false. Journalism becomes a mix of well-reported stories, exaggerated offenses, and conspiracy-laden interpretations. Often, this exposure grants such content an air of legitimacy that licenses speculation beyond the evidence. Ambulance-chasing content doesn’t always need hard facts of the full story because accusation and innuendo serve its purposes well enough.

As a matter of practice, I do not cite those who create this sort of content. I will leave it to readers to identify the ambulance chasers among so-called evangelical investigative journalists and content creators. That said, I can point to an example of ambulance chasing that may help clarify what sort of content I’m talking about. 

Consider the flare-up online when Kirk Cameron discussed annihilationism on his podcast. After the episode, numerous videos appeared referencing Cameron’s views, critiquing the annihilationist position, and framing the discussion as a scandal in ways that predictably increased channel traffic. While I would not deny that a right understanding of eternal punishment is important, it is difficult to see how a discussion on a podcast constitutes an emergency. Cameron’s show is quite popular. Without assuming that those who made response videos have no interest in combatting annihilationism, it seems clear that they also saw an opportunity to drive traffic to their channel. 

Amulance-chasing content is often self-satisfied, justifying its reporting by asserting that those it targets represent the greater evil. We must ask ourselves how such content serves to build up the body of Christ. When, for instance, do I need to know about a megachurch pastor who has been abusive, spiritually or otherwise? On one level, I want to lament with and mourn for those who have been harmed by leaders in the church. At another level, there is little I can do to effect change in that particular context. It is not that the story is irrelevant, but that I have a limited capacity to understand it, evaluate it, and speak into it faithfully. 

Perhaps this is the biggest problem with ambulance-chasing content: it’s thin. It looks only at the presenting problem while ignoring the contributing dynamics that allowed the problem to emerge. To put it another way, ambulance-chasing content has no skin-in-the-game because its creators and consumers tend to experience far more upside than downside. Even when it can be shown that such content has misconstrued facts or fabricated a scandal, its creators are largely without accountability and able to move on unscathed. Even when motivated by legitimate concerns, ambulance-chasing content subtly reshapes how Christians imagine their relationship to the church, encouraging postures of surveillance and judgment rather than participation, patience, repentance, and shared responsibility. When scandal becomes a primary means of mobilizing the body of Christ, Christians are trained to stand over against the church rather than recognizing that they are, in fact, part of it.

Third, institutionalized content seeks to retain the status quo. It resists change by limiting the questions that can be asked either through undesirable social consequences or by creating closure around specific issues. Yet the role of doctrine and faithful interpretation is to guide and form Christian judgment rather than replace it. Institutionalized content weaves assumptions into the logic of a given conversation and defines positions that organize the broader discussion.

It is important to distinguish doctrine from institutionalized content. Though both doctrine and institutionalized content create boundaries, they do not function in the same ways. Doctrine, rightly understood, provides the structure necessary for shared understanding and faithful Christian thought. The Nicene Creed, for instance, articulates positions that, if rejected, make it difficult to know what one might mean by calling themselves a Christian. Doctrine guides Christian judgment by articulating the church’s basic convictions and establishing a space within which biblical interpretation, theological thought, and general discernment may take place. 

Institutional content, by contrast, tends to cultivate loyalty to a particular “camp” or “sect” within Christianity. It often appeals to doctrine to legitimate its own positions while discrediting other plausible interpretations of the biblical text. Doctrine no longer guides Christian judgment but is used to eliminate any need for it. Specific positions become tools for enforcing alignment, narrowing the space for discernment, and creating unnecessary divisions within the body of Christ.  

While constraints are often beneficial, when those constraints are arbitrarily narrowed, disagreement or, at times, honest questioning end up marking people as deviants who do not belong with “us” but with “them.” Institutionalized content effectively dulls the double-edged sword of Scripture by dismissing possible, or even plausible, readings of the biblical text when they run against institutionalized understandings. This sort of content limits appropriate discernment and nudges, if not forces, the church away from attentive engagement with the biblical text. One interpretation becomes the only interpretation and comes to define the way Christians must live in the world. 

For example, in Recovering Biblical Manhood and Womanhood, John Piper articulates a notion of biblical masculinity that emphasizes leading, providing, and protecting women. He then anticipates a question about single mothers noting: 

“Someone might ask: So is a woman masculine if she is a single parent and provides these same things for her children? Are these only for men to do? I would answer: A woman is not unduly masculine in performing these things for her children if she has the sense that this would be properly done by her husband if she had one, and if she performs them with a uniquely feminine demeanor.”

His statement is rooted in a symbolic world characterized by a particular set of assumptions. Even so, his language is relatively ambiguous. What does it mean to be “unduly masculine,” or to perform tasks with “a uniquely feminine demeanor,” apart from the assumptions that already govern the symbolic world in which these terms operate? The phrases lack clear definition. They only appear to make sense given a specific set of assumptions.

Institutionalized content contributes to the church’s fragmentation by elevating certain readings of the biblical text—along with the assumptions that accompany them—into boundary markers for specific sects within the church. Orthodoxy and doctrinal constraints are crucial to Christian identity; however, orthodoxy can be too narrow, just as it can be too broad, when it hardens interpretive assumptions into unquestionable norms. The unquestionable norms allow institutionalized content to fragment the church by discipling Christians to prioritize their alignment with some position or set of positions (e.g., women in ministry, dispensationalism/replacement theology, young-earth creationism, etc.) while diminishing the unity of believers and undermining the coordination that faithful discipleship requires.

Activistic, ambulance-chasing, and institutionalized content differ in form and tone, but share a common effect: each coordinates Christians around a logic other than obedience to Christ. In each case, Christian language and Christian doctrine tend to be used to reinforce a shared understanding that is not trued to biblical teaching. To be fair, every piece of content misses that mark. The three types of content noted may be uniquely problematic in a digital world, but they are not the only content with problems. The solution is not an obsessive quest for perfection, but a humble, repentant posture that is neither so arrogant that revising one’s position becomes unthinkable nor so pliable that one’s mind changes every minute. Discipleship helps us develop a measured confidence that prompts ongoing discernment and questioning without collapsing into the wholesale rejection of Christian doctrine that often accompanies deconstruction. As the church’s coordinating practice, discipleship holds us together and allows us to offer a faithful, coherent witness in the midst of competing narratives. 

Discipleship as a Coordination Mechanism
For the church, discipleship functions as a coordination mechanism. As Christians learn to live under the authority of Christ, we develop a common knowledge that extends even across traditions and positions. We know that other members of Christ’s body are participating in the cruciform patter of imitating Christ. While discipleship leaves room for adaptation and diversity of Christian practice, it also forges us together as we all seek to conform more closely to Christ’s image.

Discipleship is crucial whether we are in a high-choice media environment or not. However, in a high choice media environment it helps us to avoid the sort of compartmentalization that functionally, though not formally, diminishes the authority of God’s word in realms like politics, education, economics, entertainment, work, and family. We may affirm that God’s glory is our ultimate aim, but when we shift from one domain to another, we don’t just adapt to a new context; we adopt the logics, strategies, and goals of that domain. In doing so, we “think within a frame of reference bounded by the limits of our life on earth.”

Want to change the state of the nation? Play politics. Sure, proclaim the gospel as well, but we have to make sure the right person gets elected. Want to be successful? Build influence and make money. Sure, do it as Christianly as possible, but get popular and rich. Want to make sure your kids aren’t infected by liberal values? Create a more conservative educational system. Sure, incorporate some notion of “American faith,” but make sure you cultivate conservative values. Discipleship fades into the background as we depend on the mechanisms of the world rather than living into the governing dynamics of God’s creation. These dynamics require Christians to depend on God even when doing so doesn’t seem to serve our immediate interests, needs, or desires (cf. Deut 10:12-22; 30:19-20; 1 Sam 12:14; Mal 3:10).

Discipleship teaches us to live according to the governing dynamics of God’s creation. It shapes what we believe, how we understand what we believe, and how we respond to God in the world based on that understanding. Rather than cultivating self-sufficiency, discipleship forges us into a people committed to outdoing one another in showing honor (Rom 12:10), bearing one another’s burdens (Gal 6:2), challenging one another to leave behind the ways of the flesh (Col 3:16; Heb 3:12-13; Tit 1:9, 13, 15), restoring one another to fellowship (Gal 6:1; Jam 5:19-20), confessing our sins to one another even when doing so is inconvenient (Jas 5:16; 1 Jn 1:9). We learn to exercise patience in the face of the world’s turmoil and suffering knowing that our role is not to fix the broken world but to live faithfully in a world so broken only God can fix it. 

Why This Matters
The habits we cultivate in consuming and creating content matter because individual Christian witness is never isolated from the witness of Christ’s body as a whole. We speak and act as members of the church, united together in Christ with a shared confession and a shared way of inhabiting the world, both of which are forged through discipleship. If our habits of attention and response undermine that unity, they not only shape our individual character but also disrupt the church’s coordinated proclamation in word and deed.

For this reason, Christians must ask not only whether content is factually accurate, but how it reinforces the right ordering of our loves, shapes the way we relate to one another, and fosters unity in the body of Christ. Content that regularly cultivates division, fuels suspicion, minimizes Scripture, or encourages Christians not to “outdo one another in showing honor” (Rom 12:10) should be approached with deep caution. That caution is all the more important when those creating content make it seem as if one issue or another is more urgent, righteous, or necessary than ensuring God, his word, and the teaching of the church are not reviled (1 Tim 6:1; Titus 2:5).

In a digital age, Christians must work harder to come together. We must cultivate a calm demeanor that reflects our trust in the Lord. We must be thoughtful, avoiding knee-jerk reactions that do not reflect “the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding” (Phil 4:7). We must think theologically, seeking always to glorify God so that our shared life and witness remain faithful rather than being disrupted by the habits of a digital age. 

Conclusion
Discipleship reshapes what we know, how we understand what we know, and how we respond to God from within the various situations we encounter. This latter aspect of discipleship trains us not to react to content or influencers but to respond faithfully to God. We don’t have to isolate ourselves from our media environment, though that may be wise at certain times. Instead, through discipleship, we learn to interact with content in ways that glorify the Triune God and promote the shared life of the church. We consume and create content in ways that recognize God’s presence, rather than assuming his absence or pushing him to the margins of our individual and collective lives. We will navigate our high-choice media environment faithfully only as we develop an unwavering, unreserved loyalty to God, into which the whole of our individual and collective life is immersed.
